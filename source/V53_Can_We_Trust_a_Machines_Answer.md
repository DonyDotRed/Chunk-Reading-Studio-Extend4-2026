## 0. 원문 (Full Text)

> HOST: My guest is Dr. Lena Ortiz, who studies how to evaluate AI language models. Lena, millions of people now ask these systems for medical, legal, and scientific information. Should they trust the answers?
>
> ORTIZ: Sometimes. The difficulty is knowing which times. These models are very good at producing text that sounds right. Sounding right and being right usually go together, but not always, and when they separate, the model often sounds just as confident.
>
> HOST: That's what people call hallucination?
>
> ORTIZ: Yes, though I'm not fond of the word. It suggests the system is seeing things that aren't there. What's actually happening is simpler: the model predicts what text is likely to come next, and a plausible reference or a plausible number can be very likely without being real.
>
> HOST: So how do you, as a researcher, measure trust?
>
> ORTIZ: One thing we look at is what we call calibration. A well-calibrated system is right about ninety percent of the time when it says it's ninety percent sure. Think of a weather forecast: if it says seventy percent chance of rain on a hundred days, it should rain on about seventy of them. The analogy isn't perfect, because language models don't always tell you how sure they are. But the question is the right one: does its confidence match its accuracy?
>
> HOST: What would you tell an ordinary user?
>
> ORTIZ: Three things. First, the higher the stakes, the more you should check. Second, ask for sources, and then actually open them. A citation you haven't read is just more text. And third, notice when an answer is exactly what you hoped to hear. That's when we're all least careful.
